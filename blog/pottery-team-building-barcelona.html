Pottery team building: why getting your hands dirty brings a team together

Pottery team building is an activity in which a work team shapes clay by hand for two to three hours, guided by someone with experience. Almost nobody has touched clay before, and that is exactly why it works: everyone starts from the same place, makes mistakes in front of each other and ends up helping the person next to them without being asked.

In this guide we explain why pottery works for building a team, what it has to do with leaving your comfort zone, how to organise a session and which mistakes to avoid. We write it from our studio in Barcelona, where we see all kinds of teams come through.

Why getting your hands dirty brings a team together

You cannot answer an email with clay on your hands. It sounds trivial, but it is the first thing teams notice. Phones stay on the table, face down. Attention comes back to what is happening right there: a piece taking shape, a colleague laughing because her mug has gone crooked, someone asking how you managed that rim.

Clay does not care about job titles. The director and the person who joined last month start in exactly the same place: knowing nothing. When a bowl collapses, it does not matter whose it was. What you see is who leans over to help, who shares a trick, who cheers. Those things rarely show up in a meeting.

People talk side by side, not face to face. Sitting next to each other, each looking at their own piece, conversations start on their own. No agenda, no one chairing. It is a very different kind of conversation from the one in a meeting room, and often a more honest one.

Leaving your comfort zone, without the stress

Leaving your comfort zone does not mean having a bad time. Back in 1908, psychologists Robert Yerkes and John Dodson described how a little challenge improves performance while too much pressure blocks it. Pottery sits right in that middle ground: it is new to almost everyone, but it is not scary. Nobody has to speak in public, prove anything or be fit.

What it does ask is that you get things wrong in front of others. Harvard professor Amy Edmondson studied work teams and found they learn more when members feel safe to ask questions and make mistakes without fear: she called it psychological safety. Years later, Google's Project Aristotle reached the same conclusion with its own teams. At a table full of clay, getting it wrong is normal. It is good practice for the office.

And it asks for patience. Clay has its own pace and you cannot rush it: force it and it breaks. For teams that want everything done yesterday, learning to wait is also a way out of the comfort zone.

Team building without forcing it

According to Gallup's State of the Global Workplace 2025 report, only 21% of employees worldwide felt engaged at work in 2024. An afternoon of pottery will not fix that. But it does give teams something many of them lack: time together that is not built around a target.

That is why it works best without challenges, points or winners. Everyone makes their own piece, but the table is shared: tools get passed around, bowls get compared, someone gets a round of applause when their cup finally stands up. At the end, when all the pieces are put together, the group usually stays a while just looking at them. And the memory does not stay in a photo: it ends up on a shelf at home or on a desk at work.

How to organise a pottery team building session

1. Choose the technique: hand-building or the wheel

For a team, the most practical option is hand-building: the whole group works at the same table at the same time, it takes minutes to learn and everyone can make a simple piece such as a bowl, a plate or a mug. The pottery wheel is more spectacular, but it needs a machine and one-to-one attention per person, so it suits very small groups.

2. Pick the place: a studio or your office

Going to a studio has one clear advantage: taking the team out of its usual setting changes the mood from the first minute. Holding it at the office or another venue allows bigger groups and saves travel. All you need is tables, a floor that can be cleaned and access to water.

3. Plan the time

Two to three hours is the length that works best. Any less and the team barely gets past the explanation. Any more and tiredness kicks in before enthusiasm. Keep a few minutes at the end to look at the pieces together: that is when the group sees itself as a group.

4. Know what happens to the pieces

Pieces do not go home on the day. The clay has to dry completely and is then fired in a kiln at high temperature, usually twice, with glazing in between. The whole process takes a few weeks. Knowing this in advance avoids surprises and gives the team a reason to get together again when the pieces arrive.

5. What to ask before booking

  • Whether materials, tools and firing are included in the price.
  • How many people run the session and for what group size.
  • Which languages it is run in, if your team is international.
  • When and how the fired pieces are handed over.
  • Whether they can come to your office and what space they need.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Turning it into a competition. Picking "the best piece" brings back the pressure the activity is meant to remove.
  • Expecting perfect pieces. Irregular shapes are normal the first time, and they are often the ones people like most later.
  • Squeezing it into an hour. Rushed, the experience turns into a demonstration.
  • Forgetting about clothes. Clay washes out easily, but tell the team to come in comfortable clothes.
  • Making it compulsory. It works better when people arrive curious rather than out of obligation.

Frequently asked questions

What is pottery team building?

It is a group activity in which a work team shapes clay by hand for two to three hours, guided by people with ceramics experience. Each participant makes their own piece, which is then dried and fired in a kiln.

Do we need any experience?

No. Hand-building takes a few minutes to learn and is designed for beginners. The fact that nobody knows how is part of why it works: the whole team starts from the same place.

Is hand-building or the pottery wheel better for a team?

For groups, hand-building. The whole team works at the same table at the same time and nobody is left waiting. The wheel needs a machine and one-to-one attention per person, so it suits very small groups.

Why can we not take our pieces home the same day?

Because the clay has to dry completely and be fired in a kiln at high temperature, usually twice. The process takes a few weeks. After that, everyone collects their piece or has it delivered.

Can pottery team building take place at our office?

Yes. All you need is tables, a space that can get a little messy and access to water. Whoever runs the session brings the clay and tools and takes the pieces away to fire them. At La Mesa we do this for groups of up to 40 people.

How many people can take part?

It depends on the space and how many people are running the session. At our studio in La Barceloneta we work with groups of up to 10 people; away from the studio, at an office or another venue, with groups of up to 40.
